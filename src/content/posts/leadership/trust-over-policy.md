> “Hospital mein ab jo karna hai woh doctor ko karna hai, what will this person do in the hospital?”
>
> Translation: “Whatever has to be done in the hospital now is the doctor’s job. What will this person do there?”

Early in my career, I led a support team. One day a team member who reported to me asked for two days of leave. Their mother had been admitted to hospital. Approving leave was my call, so I approved it.

A little later my manager found out, and brought it up when we were talking. He didn’t overturn the decision. He questioned it, and that line above was how he put it.

I was shocked. Not because he raised his voice or made a scene, but because of how he saw the situation. To him, a family emergency was a question of whether the person would be productive at the hospital.

## Technically right, completely wrong

My team member couldn’t treat their mother. They couldn’t speed up a diagnosis. On a spreadsheet, their being at the hospital added nothing, and their being away left a gap in the queue.

But leave during a family emergency isn’t about being useful. It’s about being there. Sitting in a waiting room, talking to doctors, being in the room when your mother wakes up: none of that shows up as output, and all of it matters more than any ticket.

## Policy is the floor, not the decision

Since then I’ve seen the same pattern many times. Someone applies a cost-benefit test to a situation it was never meant for, and treats the answer as obvious.

Policies and metrics are built for the average case. They exist so routine decisions don’t need a debate every time. But a team lead’s job isn’t to apply policy. If that were the job, a form could do it. The job is to use judgment where the policy runs out, and a person in crisis is almost always past that point.

That’s what I mean by trust over policy. When I have to choose between trusting someone who tells me they need to be somewhere and protecting two days of coverage, I trust the person.

## How I approach it: the team covers, the person goes

For me, the right first answer to a personal emergency is simply “go.” Everything else comes after.

Then the work becomes a team problem, not the absent person’s problem. Sit down with the rest of the team and plan coverage together: who takes the open tickets, who handles escalations, which commitments need a handover and which can wait. The person leaving should only need to flag anything urgent. They shouldn’t be answering messages from a hospital corridor.

Handling it this way does two things.

First, the person comes back without guilt or a backlog waiting for them. They return ready to work, not already behind.

Second, the rest of the team is watching. Everyone who helps cover learns that when their own crisis comes, the team will do the same for them. That’s not a perk. It’s the foundation of everything else you ask of people.

## Why this matters for support teams in particular

Support work depends on effort nobody can force: staying calm with an angry customer, writing the extra paragraph that prevents a follow-up ticket, stepping up when volume spikes without being asked. You can’t put that in a policy. People give it when they believe the organisation is on their side.

When a manager treats a hospitalised parent as a productivity question, that attitude doesn’t stay in one conversation. It shapes how decisions get made, and teams pick up on it. It shows up later in attrition, disengagement, and people doing exactly what’s required and nothing more.

## Trust isn’t the same as no rules

To be fair to the other side: judgment without guardrails turns into favouritism. If emergency leave is right for one person, it’s right for everyone in the same situation, and the coverage plan is how the work still gets done. Trusting people doesn’t mean ignoring the business. It means remembering that the business is made of people, and they remember how they were treated on their worst days.

I’ve never forgotten that conversation. It’s the moment I decided what kind of leader I wanted to be.
