Most support interviews test the wrong thing. They reward people who talk well about technology, for a job that is mostly writing clearly to someone who is frustrated. AI hasn’t made that less important. As AI handles more of the simple tickets, the frustrated customers are increasingly the ones who reach a person.

I was closely involved in hiring as a support manager at Deloitte and Teradata, on the side of the team that needed the hires. At Automattic, I saw the other side, spending six months on the hiring team as a recruiter. Having done both showed me what each side needs, and where the process usually falls short.

Here’s what I’d tell anyone hiring support engineers: what to look for, how to run the interview, and the mistakes I see most often.

## Decide what you’re hiring for first

If you can’t name what a great hire looks like before the interview, you’ll end up hiring whoever you liked most.

For support engineers, I look for five things:

1. **Clear writing.** Most support happens in writing, and the customer only reads your words. AI can draft a polite reply in seconds, so fluent writing alone isn’t the skill. The skill is knowing what a good reply looks like, and spotting when a draft, yours or the AI’s, misses the point.
2. **Troubleshooting.** Not knowing every answer, but knowing how to narrow a problem down.
3. **Empathy that leads to action.** Acknowledging the frustration in one honest line, then fixing the problem.
4. **Ownership.** Following a problem through when the answer isn’t in the docs.
5. **Learning speed.** Your product will change. Their current knowledge matters less than how fast they pick things up.

Notice what isn’t on the list: deep knowledge of your product. You can teach that in weeks. The five above are much harder to teach.

## Best practices

### 1. Test writing with real writing

A support engineer’s main tool is the written reply, so make them write one. Give candidates two or three realistic tickets: a confused beginner, a technical bug report and an angry customer. Then read the replies the way a customer would.

Give them real time to do it well. A lot of companies now give candidates tasks they can finish over a weekend or across a whole week. That lets them work around their current job and at their own pace, the way they’d actually work on a remote team. What matters is a clear scope and a fair deadline, not how fast it’s done.

These are practice tickets, not real customer work, so they’re usually unpaid. Paid work comes later, in the trial (practice 10).

Expect candidates to use AI on a take-home task, and tell them upfront whether it’s allowed. If it is, test their judgment in a live follow-up: ask why they chose that tone, what they left out, and how they’d change the reply for a different customer. If it isn’t, add a short live writing exercise so you see their own writing.

### 2. Use real scenarios, not trivia

“What does a 502 error mean?” checks memory. “A customer says their site went down after they changed DNS an hour ago. What do you do?” checks thinking.

Pull your scenarios from real tickets your team has solved, with the details changed.

### 3. Watch how they troubleshoot, not just what they conclude

Ask them to think out loud. The best candidates ask questions before they offer fixes: When did it start? What changed? Is it happening to everyone?

A wrong answer reached through a sound process is a better sign than a right answer that was a lucky guess.

### 4. Ask about past behaviour, then dig

“Tell me about a time you handled an upset customer” is a fine start. The value is in the follow-ups: What exactly did you write? What would you do differently? How did it end?

Vague answers usually stay vague when you push. Real experience gets more specific.

### 5. Use the same structure for every candidate

Ask every candidate the same core questions and score them against the same criteria. Without that, you’re comparing one candidate’s good day with another’s awkward first five minutes.

### 6. Score on your own before the debrief

Each interviewer should write down a score and evidence before anyone talks. Otherwise the most senior or the loudest person sets the verdict, and everyone else nods.

### 7. Be honest about the hard parts of the job

Tell candidates about queue pressure, weekend or shift coverage, and repetitive tickets. People who hear it upfront and still want the job tend to stay.

### 8. Hire for the team’s time zones too

For distributed teams, ask how they hand work over and how they work without instant answers. In APAC, support teams often cover the hours when other regions are offline, so check early that the candidate can work that schedule.

### 9. Treat every candidate like a customer

Reply on time, explain the process, and give the people you reject a clear answer. Your hiring process is the first support experience a candidate has with your team.

### 10. If you run a trial, make it fair

Many companies now add a trial before a full-time offer: a paid project lasting a few days or weeks, or a short contract before a permanent role. I’ve led candidates through paid trials myself, and done well, it’s the most reliable signal you’ll get, because you see real work on real problems instead of interview answers.

Done badly, it drives good people away. A few rules:

- **Pay for it.** A trial means handling real customers, which makes it real work. Asking for that unpaid is unfair, and the strongest candidates often say no.
- **Keep it flexible.** Most good candidates already have a job. Agree on a time box upfront and let them work around it.
- **Say what you’re judging.** Tell them what a successful trial looks like before they start.
- **Give them a real contact.** Someone who answers questions quickly and gives feedback along the way.
- **Close it properly.** Every candidate gets a clear decision and honest feedback, whether you hire them or not. Some recruiters and hiring managers disagree, because of the volume and back-and-forth it can create. But one clear message still goes a long way toward showing your company at its best.

A trial comes after the writing exercise in practice 1. The exercise uses practice tickets and decides who gets a trial. The trial uses real ones and decides who gets an offer.

## Common mistakes

**Hiring for product knowledge over learning ability.** A candidate who already knows your stack looks safe. A candidate who learns fast will overtake them within a quarter.

**Judging a writing job by how people talk.** Some excellent support engineers are quiet on a video call and brilliant in writing. If the live interview carries most of the weight, you’ll miss them.

**Taking confidence for competence.** A quick, confident answer can still be wrong. In support, “Let me check that before I answer” is a strength, not a weakness.

**Gotcha questions.** Trick questions show you who has seen the trick before. They tell you nothing about how someone treats a customer.

**Hiring people like yourself.** “Culture fit” often turns into “reminds me of me”. Ask instead what this person would add that the team is missing.

**Talking more than listening.** If the interviewer speaks for most of the hour, you’ve learned very little. Aim to talk for 20–30% of the time at most.

I made this mistake myself early in my career. I did most of the talking in interviews, and it led to some bad hires because I wasn’t learning enough to judge candidates well. Once I noticed, I changed the habit: candidates did most of the talking, and I saved my part for the end. I judged people more accurately, and candidates got a fairer chance to show what they could do.

**Untrained interviewers.** Two interviewers can hold very different standards for the same answer. Calibrate them first: score a sample answer together and talk through the differences.

**A slow or silent process.** Strong candidates have options. Weeks of silence lose them, and the people you reject without an answer remember how you treated them.

**Skipping the hard tickets.** A candidate who handles a polite how-to question well may freeze on a refund dispute or a security scare. Test with the tickets that actually decide whether a customer stays.

## A simple scorecard to start with

Score each trait from 1 to 4 and write down the evidence beside the score. A score with no evidence doesn’t count.

| Trait | What good looks like | A question to ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clear writing | Short, plain, answers the real question, the next step is obvious | Written exercise on three realistic tickets, then a live follow-up on their choices |
| Troubleshooting | Asks before fixing, narrows down step by step, checks the fix | “A customer’s site went down after a DNS change. Walk me through it.” |
| Empathy that leads to action | Acknowledges once, then solves; no scripted apologies | “Rewrite this curt reply for a customer on their third contact.” |
| Ownership | Follows through, escalates with full context, closes the loop | “Tell me about a problem you saw through when it wasn’t your job.” |
| Learning speed | Picks up something unfamiliar quickly, admits gaps | “Here’s a tool you haven’t used. Ten minutes: how would you help a customer with it?” |

## The bottom line

A good support interview process looks like the job: real tickets, real writing, real customer problems. Decide what good looks like before you meet anyone, score against the evidence rather than your gut, and treat candidates the way you’d want your team to treat customers.
