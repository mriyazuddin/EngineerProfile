Recently, while managing an online store for someone, I ran into a routine admin task on the shipping platform the store uses. Two orders had already been fulfilled and shipped outside that platform. All I needed was to mark those two orders as completed in the dashboard, so the records stayed clean.

What followed was a clear lesson in how support goes wrong, with AI and with humans.

## The trap of unthinking automation

When I opened the support chat and asked my question, the bot immediately pushed me toward generating shipment IDs. Assuming this was a required step to close the orders, I followed along. It turned out to charge the store’s account for shipping on orders that had already shipped.

When I went back to fix it and get a real answer, the chat bounced me between bots and live agents.

## When humans act like bots

You expect an untrained bot to miss nuance. You don’t expect a human support specialist to do the same.

Even after I explained that the orders had shipped outside their platform and I only wanted to close them, the live agent replied with a copy-pasted script on how to schedule a pickup. When I asked whether they had read my message, they doubled down, politely acknowledging my concern and asking me, once again, to schedule the pickup myself.

Minutes later, another prompt asked for the shipment ID all over again. Two minutes after that, the chat was auto-closed for inactivity. By then I had given up on getting an answer, so I fixed it myself: I cancelled the shipments and orders on the shipping platform and left the orders in WooCommerce as they were. It wasn’t clean, but it was the best I could do.

Nobody stopped to ask: what is this person actually trying to do? They saw the word “orders”, reached for the shipping playbook, and forced my problem into their script.

## The gold standard: listening first

This reminded me of a call with American Express years ago, when I had a question about enrolling in their rewards program. It’s still the best support I’ve ever had.

The difference wasn’t speed, and it wasn’t fancy tech. It was a simple, disciplined approach:

- **Clarify the goal.** The representative first asked what I was trying to achieve.
- **Confirm understanding.** They repeated my goal back in their own words, to make sure we were on the same page.
- **Anticipate what’s next.** Once they had solved my question, they thought one step ahead and dealt with follow-up issues I hadn’t realized were coming.

I hung up that call feeling taken care of. I left the shipping platform’s chat with my question unanswered and the store’s account charged for shipping it didn’t need. Thankfully, the platform refunded the charges automatically once I cancelled the shipments and orders.

## What I’d change as a support leader

It’s tempting to blame the agents, but they’re usually doing exactly what their system rewards. If I were running this team, I’d start with four things:

- **Let agents go off-script.** A script is a starting point. When a customer’s problem doesn’t fit it, agents should be trained, and trusted, to say so and deal with the real question.
- **Pass on the goal, not just the transcript.** When a bot hands a chat to a person, the first thing the agent sees should be what the customer is trying to do.
- **Measure resolution, not just speed.** If agents are judged mainly on response times, you’ll get fast replies that ignore the question. Even where fast responses matter, let agents tell the customer they need a minute to read the conversation so far.
- **Confirm the real issue first.** Taking a few seconds to identify what the customer actually needs, and repeating it back to them, makes sure you’re solving the right problem.

Bots need the same discipline. Before a bot suggests an action that costs the customer money, it should check that the action matches what the customer actually asked for.

## The takeaway

Companies invest heavily in chatbots and strict SLA timers to make support faster. But speed means nothing if you’re running in the wrong direction.

Whether it’s an AI agent or a person behind the keyboard, good support comes down to one rule: understand the goal before you offer the solution. Until businesses train their teams, and their bots, to read and listen, “live support” will remain just another frustrating loop.
